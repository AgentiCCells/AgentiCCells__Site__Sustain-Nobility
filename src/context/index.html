Context, with sources

The rules and standards behind "governed responsibly"

An SME asked to prove its AI is governed responsibly will meet a small set of laws, guidance and standards. This page lists the main ones, each fact with its source.

This page is AgentiCCells research, not SUSTAIN-NOBILITY's words, and not legal advice. The facts below were written from AgentiCCells's notes and have not yet been re-read against the sources on 2026-11-18; the researcher re-checks each one in the first round. Rules in this area change: always check the source before relying on a date.

Some statements on this page are still unchecked or marked "to be confirmed", including the AI Act dates after the 2026 Omnibus. For the dates read from the sources on 2026-11-18, each with its link, see the research page, section on the field.

European Union: the AI Act

The EU Artificial Intelligence Act is Regulation (EU) 2024/1689. It was published in the Official Journal of the EU on 12 July 2024 and entered into force on 1 August 2024.

Source: EUR-Lex, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj. Dates as in the published text.

It applies in stages (Article 113): the prohibited practices and the AI literacy duty from 2 February 2025; the rules on general-purpose AI models, governance and penalties from 2 August 2025; most remaining provisions from 2 August 2026; and the classification rule for high-risk AI in products covered by other EU safety law (Article 6(1)) from 2 August 2027.

Source: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 113, EUR-Lex.

The Act can reach organisations outside the EU: it covers providers and deployers established in a third country where the output of their AI system is used in the EU (Article 2).

Source: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 2(1)(c), EUR-Lex.

In November 2025 the European Commission proposed changes to the AI Act's timetable for high-risk systems as part of a "digital omnibus" package. Whether and in what form these were adopted must be checked before relying on the dates above.

Source: European Commission, AI Act policy page, digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu. Status: to be confirmed by the researcher.

International standard: ISO/IEC 42001

ISO/IEC 42001:2023 sets requirements for an AI management system: how an organisation establishes, runs and improves its governance of AI. It was published in December 2023 and is designed so organisations can be certified against it.

Source: ISO, ISO/IEC 42001:2023, iso.org/standard/81230.html.

United Kingdom: a principles-based approach

In March 2023 the UK government published the white paper "A pro-innovation approach to AI regulation". It set out five cross-sector principles for existing regulators to apply within their remits: safety, security and robustness; appropriate transparency and explainability; fairness; accountability and governance; and contestability and redress.

Source: Department for Science, Innovation and Technology, GOV.UK, gov.uk/government/publications/ai-regulation-a-pro-innovation-approach, March 2023.

The government published its response to the white paper consultation in February 2024, keeping the principles-based, regulator-led approach.

Source: GOV.UK, "A pro-innovation approach to AI regulation: government response", gov.uk consultation page, February 2024. UK policy may have moved since; the researcher checks the current position.

The Information Commissioner's Office publishes guidance on AI and data protection, covering how UK data protection law applies when AI processes personal data.

Source: ICO, guidance on artificial intelligence, ico.org.uk.

Widely used framework: NIST AI RMF

The US National Institute of Standards and Technology published the AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0) in January 2023. It is voluntary and organises AI risk work into four functions: govern, map, measure and manage.

Source: NIST, AI Risk Management Framework, nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework.
